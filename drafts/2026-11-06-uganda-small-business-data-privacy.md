# A Customer List Is Personal Data: A Practical Privacy Guide for Uganda’s Small Businesses

**By Kastine Augak · 6 November 2026**

*A phone full of customer contacts, WhatsApp orders and payment records can create real privacy responsibilities. Here is a practical starting point for Ugandan micro and small businesses.*

A small business does not need a giant customer database to hold information that deserves protection. A shopkeeper may have names and phone numbers in a notebook. A salon may use WhatsApp to confirm appointments. A mobile-money agent may handle transaction references. A school, clinic, online seller or delivery service may collect addresses, photographs, identification details or information about a person's health.

Once information can identify an individual, it may be personal data. The fact that it is stored on a phone, in a spreadsheet or in a paper file does not make the responsibility disappear.

Uganda already has a legal framework for this. The **Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019**, together with its 2021 Regulations, applies to people and organisations that collect, process, hold or use personal data in Uganda. It can also apply to organisations outside Uganda handling personal data relating to Ugandan citizens. The practical message is straightforward: privacy should be part of ordinary business operations, not something considered only after a breach.

## Start by recognising the data you already hold

Personal data is broader than a national identity number. It can include a person's name, phone number, email address, location, occupation, photograph, account identifier or information that can be combined with other details to identify them.

For a Ugandan small business, common examples include:

- customer names and phone numbers used for orders or deliveries;
- WhatsApp conversations and appointment histories;
- employee contacts, payroll details and job records;
- customer photographs, CCTV footage or voice recordings;
- mobile-money transaction references linked to a person;
- website forms, newsletter subscriptions and customer-support messages.

The first useful exercise is not buying software. It is making a simple list of what information the business collects, why it collects it, where it is stored and who can see it.

A business that keeps customer details in several staff members' personal phones has a different risk from one that stores them in a controlled business account. Both need to understand what they hold.

## Know whether you are the controller or the processor

The Act distinguishes between a **data controller**, which determines why and how personal data is processed, and a **data processor**, which processes information on behalf of a controller.

If a shop collects a customer's phone number to arrange delivery, the shop will commonly be deciding the purpose of that collection. If it uses an external service to host its customer-management system, that service may be processing information on the shop's behalf. The exact role depends on the real arrangement, not just the label in a contract.

This distinction matters because responsibility cannot be outsourced simply by using a cloud service or an online form. A business should understand what its service providers do with the information, what security measures they use, whether they use subcontractors and how information can be returned or deleted when the service ends.

## Check the Personal Data Protection Office registration requirement

Section 29 of the Data Protection and Privacy Act establishes a Data Protection Register. The Personal Data Protection Office (PDPO) says organisations that collect or process personal data must register and obtain a certificate. The Government of Uganda's Doing Business portal currently describes the requirement broadly, covering data collectors, controllers and processors, including individuals, organisations and public bodies.

Do not assume that a small turnover, a home-based business or a small contact list automatically creates an exemption. The official government portal says no exemptions have been gazetted, but businesses should check the current position and their exact circumstances directly with the PDPO before relying on an exemption or making a payment.

The same portal lists an annual registration fee of UGX 100,000 and says registration is renewed yearly. That is the amount shown on the portal when it was last reviewed on 15 June 2026; confirm the current fee, process and documents with the PDPO before paying. Use the official PDPO registration route rather than an intermediary who promises to register a business on your behalf.

The PDPO also published a Data Protection and Privacy Compliance Toolkit for organisations on 30 July 2026. It is a useful starting point for understanding the regulator's expectations and finding practical resources.

## Collect information for a clear reason—and no more than you need

The Act's data-protection principles require accountability, fair and lawful processing, adequate and relevant information, limits on unnecessary collection and retention, data quality, transparency and security safeguards.

That means a business should be able to answer a simple question about every field on a form: **Why do we need this?**

A delivery service may need a customer's name, phone number and delivery location. It may not need the customer's national ID number for an ordinary delivery. A newsletter may need an email address, but it does not automatically need a date of birth, home address or access to a phone's contacts.

Avoid collecting information simply because a form builder makes it easy to add another field. Extra data creates extra responsibility. It can also make a business less trustworthy.

## Explain what you are doing with the information

The Act generally requires prior consent for collecting or processing personal data, while also providing specific circumstances in which processing may be authorised or necessary—for example, to perform a contract or comply with a legal obligation. Consent is therefore important, but it is not the only possible lawful basis.

A practical privacy notice should tell customers, in language they can understand:

1. who is collecting the information;
2. what information is being collected;
3. why it is needed;
4. whether it will be shared with another service provider;
5. how long it will be kept, where that can be explained;
6. how a person can ask questions or exercise their rights.

Do not hide important information in a long paragraph that customers are unlikely to read. Put a short explanation beside the form, subscription box or point of collection. Do not treat a customer's phone number given for one delivery as automatic permission to add them to unrelated marketing messages.

The Act also gives individuals ways to request correction or deletion of information that is inaccurate, excessive, irrelevant, out of date, incomplete, misleading or no longer lawfully retained. Businesses should have a real person and a workable process for handling such requests.

## Protect phones, accounts and shared files

A privacy policy is not a security control. The Act requires appropriate technical and organisational measures to protect personal data against loss, damage, unauthorised destruction, unlawful access and unauthorised processing.

For a small team, practical safeguards can begin with a few manageable steps:

- use a different, strong password for each important business account;
- turn on multi-factor authentication wherever it is available;
- lock phones and computers, and keep operating systems and apps updated;
- remove access when a staff member leaves or changes roles;
- avoid sending customer lists to personal email accounts or public group chats;
- limit shared spreadsheets to people who genuinely need them;
- keep a secure backup and test that it can be restored;
- avoid storing payment PINs, passwords or unnecessary identity documents.

If a service provider processes personal data for the business, section 21 requires attention to the processor's security measures and to contractual arrangements that protect confidentiality and security. A business should not assume that a familiar brand name or a free account automatically makes a service suitable for sensitive information.

## Think carefully about cloud storage outside Uganda

Many small businesses use email, online forms, cloud drives, customer-management tools and website hosts whose infrastructure may be outside Uganda. The Act does not mean that every overseas service is automatically prohibited. Section 19 sets conditions for processing or storing personal data outside Uganda, including adequate protection in the destination country or the data subject's consent.

Before uploading customer information, find out what the provider's terms say about storage locations, subcontractors, security, access and deletion. If the service cannot explain how it protects the data, do not upload sensitive customer records until the issue is resolved. For complex or high-risk arrangements, obtain qualified legal advice.

## Prepare for a breach before one happens

A lost phone, compromised email account, exposed spreadsheet or wrongly shared customer list can become a data-protection incident. The PDPO's public portal instructs organisations to report data breaches that may compromise personal data immediately.

Every business should know who will act if something goes wrong. A basic response plan should identify the person responsible, the accounts that need to be secured, how to preserve relevant records, how to contact the PDPO and how to communicate with affected people in line with the regulator's instructions and applicable law.

Do not delete evidence in a panic, quietly reuse a compromised password or assume that a small incident does not matter. Contain the problem, document what is known, seek appropriate support and follow the reporting requirements.

## A practical 30-day starting plan

A small business can make meaningful progress without buying an expensive compliance platform.

**Week 1 — Map the information.** List the personal data collected, the purpose for each item, the storage locations and the people or providers with access.

**Week 2 — Reduce and explain.** Remove fields that are not needed. Write a short privacy notice and explain how customers can contact the business about their information.

**Week 3 — Secure access.** Enable multi-factor authentication, review shared accounts, update devices, limit access and check backups.

**Week 4 — Check obligations and suppliers.** Review the PDPO registration process, examine cloud and processor arrangements, assign a responsible contact and write a simple breach-response checklist.

The PDPO's compliance toolkit and the #BeeraSharp Digital Rights and Security Toolkit for Ugandan Businesses from the Collaboration on International ICT Policy for East and Southern Africa (CIPESA) provide further guidance. The CIPESA resource includes checklists on data protection, passwords, multi-factor authentication, device security, mobile money, cloud services and incident response.

## Privacy is part of customer service

Customers share information because they expect a business to use it for a clear purpose and protect it from misuse. A small enterprise that explains its practices, collects less information and secures its accounts is not only reducing legal risk; it is building confidence.

The useful question is not whether a business is large enough to care about privacy. It is whether it can explain what it knows about its customers, why it needs that information, who can access it and what happens when something goes wrong.

For Uganda's digital businesses, that is a practical foundation for trust.

## Practical takeaways

- Treat names, phone numbers, addresses, photos and linked transaction records as potentially personal data.
- Map what the business collects and why before buying another digital tool.
- Check the current PDPO registration requirements and fees directly with the regulator.
- Use clear notices, collect only what is necessary and set a retention period.
- Secure accounts with unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, updates and limited access.
- Review cloud-provider and processor arrangements, especially when data is stored outside Uganda.
- Prepare a breach-response process and follow PDPO reporting instructions.

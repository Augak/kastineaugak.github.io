# East Africa’s AI strategy is adopted. The hard work begins now

*EAC ministers approved regional AI and digital transformation strategies in Kampala on 31 October 2026, alongside a university AI module and Kiswahili certification benchmarks. The next question is whether institutions can turn policy into practical skills, infrastructure and locally useful tools.*

**By Kastine Augak**  
**Editorial status:** Research-led draft for editorial review; not published.

## A regional decision made in Kampala

East Africa has taken a significant policy step on artificial intelligence. At a ministerial session held in Kampala from 27 to 31 October 2026, ministers responsible for education, science and technology, culture and sports in the East African Community (EAC) adopted regional strategies for Artificial Intelligence and Digital Transformation, as well as a Biotechnology Strategy.

The EAC’s announcement, published on 31 October, says the strategies are intended to provide a shared framework for technology development and application in areas including education, agriculture, healthcare, industry and public administration. The decisions were made at the 21st Sectoral Council on Education, Science and Technology, Culture and Sports, hosted at the new headquarters of the Inter-University Council for East Africa (IUCEA) in Kampala.

This is more than a regional headline. It is a signal that AI is being treated as an education, economic-development and governance issue—not only as a collection of software products.

## What was actually approved?

The ministerial session approved an AI foundational module for higher education. The module is intended to equip undergraduate students with essential AI knowledge and practical competencies regardless of their academic discipline. Partner States were asked to consider adopting it across university degree programmes, while IUCEA was tasked with developing a regional implementation framework.

Ministers also approved the establishment and operationalisation of a Regional Artificial Intelligence Network of Universities. The stated purpose is to strengthen cooperation in AI education, research and innovation across the region. In addition, the Council endorsed regional instruments for teacher education covering curriculum and competency, institutional policy, and codes of conduct.

A further decision concerns language. The ministers directed the development of AI certification benchmarks for Kiswahili. That is an important part of the region’s digital inclusion discussion: AI systems should be assessed for how well they serve the languages and communities that people actually use.

The EAC statement also says the ministers adopted the EAC Kiswahili Language Policy and recommended it for consideration by the Council of Ministers. That is a separate procedural step; it should not be described as final approval by the Council of Ministers.

## Approval is not the same as implementation

The announcement confirms that the strategies and foundational module were adopted or approved. It does not say that all universities have already introduced the module. Partner States were directed to consider adoption, and IUCEA still has to develop the regional implementation framework.

Nor does the public statement set out a detailed rollout timetable or a specific new budget for every action. In fact, it acknowledges financial and resource constraints affecting implementation. That makes the next stage especially important.

A credible rollout will need trained lecturers, adaptable teaching materials, reliable electricity and connectivity, affordable access to devices, computing resources for research, and quality datasets. It will also need rules that protect personal information, make institutions accountable and keep human judgement in the loop where decisions affect people’s lives.

The region should avoid treating AI literacy as a narrow course about prompting chatbots. Students need to understand how automated systems work at a basic level, where errors and bias can arise, how to check sources, when not to share sensitive information and why some decisions should remain under qualified human supervision.

## Uganda has a practical role to play

The ministerial meeting took place in Uganda, but the decisions apply to the EAC’s eight Partner States: Burundi, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kenya, Rwanda, Somalia, South Sudan, Tanzania and Uganda. A shared regional framework could help universities collaborate, researchers exchange knowledge and innovators build services for a wider market.

Uganda also has a national cybersecurity context to consider. On 15 July 2026, the National Information Technology Authority–Uganda (NITA-U) announced the updated National Information Security Framework 2026. The framework introduces practical assessment tools, stronger governance requirements and baseline security controls for critical information infrastructure and operational technology.

That work is relevant because AI systems are increasingly connected to organisational data, public services and critical operations. Regional AI ambitions should be matched by secure systems, clear access controls, incident response and responsible data handling.

The broader African ecosystem is also trying to build local capacity. On 2 October 2026, the United Nations Development Programme and the GSMA announced a partnership to support African-led AI innovation, including African-language solutions and improved access to computing infrastructure for priority pilots. This is useful context for the region, although it should not be mistaken for a guarantee that every local project has funding or is already deployed.

## What should universities, businesses and innovators do next?

Universities can begin by mapping existing courses, identifying lecturer-training needs and testing how foundational AI concepts can fit into different disciplines. An agriculture student, a law student and a computer-science student do not need identical assignments, but all should learn to assess outputs, protect data and understand the limits of automated systems.

Small businesses do not need to wait for a regional strategy to become useful. They can start with one low-risk workflow: drafting a product description, organising non-sensitive notes or preparing a first version of a customer FAQ. Before using any tool, owners should check its data-retention rules, usage limits and costs after any free tier. Sensitive customer records should not be pasted into a service without a clear legal and security basis.

Developers and researchers should start with local problems and involve the people affected. Language technology needs language experts and community participation. Agriculture tools need farmers’ input. Health applications need qualified professionals and strong safeguards. A technically impressive prototype is not the same as a reliable public service.

## Five signals to watch

Over the coming months, readers should look for five practical developments:

1. **An IUCEA implementation framework** with responsibilities, timelines and a realistic plan for different university environments.
2. **University pilots and lecturer preparation** rather than announcements alone.
3. **Transparent Kiswahili AI benchmarks** developed with language specialists and users.
4. **Sustainable infrastructure and funding** for connectivity, power, computing and research data.
5. **Privacy, cybersecurity and accountability measures** built into education and deployment from the beginning.

These are not claims that the EAC has already completed these steps. They are a practical scorecard for judging whether the policy decisions are turning into public value.

## The real measure is usefulness

The adoption of regional AI and digital transformation strategies gives East Africa a common direction. But the success of that direction will not be measured by the number of strategy documents or AI demonstrations. It will be measured by whether students gain useful skills, whether local innovators can build sustainable services, whether institutions protect people’s information and whether communities see tangible improvements.

For Uganda and its neighbours, the opportunity is to shape AI around local languages, local needs and local accountability. The next chapter should be about delivery.

**A question for readers:** If East Africa could prioritise one area first, should it be AI skills, local-language technology or the infrastructure that makes both possible?

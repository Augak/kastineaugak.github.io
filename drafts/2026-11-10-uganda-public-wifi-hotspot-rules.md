# Uganda’s Public Wi-Fi Debate: Can Regulation Protect Users Without Pricing Out Small Hotspots?

*UCC is consulting on licensing options after warning about unlicensed hotspot operations. Parliament has heard calls for affordable compliance. The test is whether Uganda can make public Wi-Fi safer while keeping neighbourhood access within reach.*

**By Kastine Augak**

Public Wi-Fi hotspots have become part of everyday connectivity in many Ugandan neighbourhoods, trading centres, student areas and small businesses. For some customers, paying a small amount to connect for a short period is more practical than maintaining an individual data bundle or fixed broadband subscription.

That business model is now at the centre of a regulatory dispute. The Uganda Communications Commission (UCC) says public telecommunications services must be provided under the appropriate authorisation. Small hotspot resellers and their representatives want a compliance route that recognises their role in extending access without imposing costs that make the business impossible.

The issue is not simply whether regulation is necessary. It is how regulation can protect customers, networks and public infrastructure while allowing legitimate small businesses to operate.

## What UCC says is happening

In a press release published on 15 October 2026, UCC said it had identified approximately 4,000 unlicensed public Wi-Fi providers across Uganda. The Commission said some operators buy fixed broadband, fibre-to-the-home or fixed wireless access from licensed providers and then resell connectivity to the public without the required licence.

UCC also estimated that the activity was associated with about UGX 148 billion in annual lost tax revenue. That is the regulator’s estimate; the public statement does not provide an independently audited calculation. The Commission raised concerns about consumer protection, network interference, equipment installed on electricity poles or other infrastructure without permission, and limited traceability when incidents occur.

These are legitimate regulatory questions. Customers need to know who is responsible for the service they buy, what quality they can expect, how their information is handled and where to complain if something goes wrong. Network equipment also needs to be installed safely and in ways that do not interfere with other infrastructure.

However, the existence of those risks does not by itself answer what a workable licensing model should look like for a small operator serving one arcade, hostel or neighbourhood.

## The traders’ case is about affordability and livelihoods

The Kampala City Traders Association (KACITA) took the dispute to Parliament’s Committee on Information, Communication Technology and National Guidance on 28 October.

Parliament’s report says KACITA told the committee that enforcement was affecting hotspot operators and suppliers of networking equipment. The association referred to approximately 40,000 operators and asked for licensing requirements that small businesses could realistically meet.

That figure should not be treated as a verified national census. It is KACITA’s estimate, while UCC’s earlier public estimate was approximately 4,000 unlicensed providers. The two figures may use different definitions of an operator, reseller, supplier or individual hotspot. A transparent registration exercise is needed before either number is used as a definitive count.

The difference matters. Policy based on an unclear count can produce the wrong fee structure, enforcement priorities or support programme.

KACITA’s wider argument is that neighbourhood hotspots serve people who may not be able to afford a separate internet subscription. That does not exempt a business from the law, but it is a reason to consider whether a proportionate route into compliance is possible.

## What has changed since the enforcement announcement?

UCC’s public position has developed through a series of meetings.

On 20 October, the Commission said it had met KACITA and announced a multi-stakeholder committee to examine possible licensing arrangements. It also said a registration exercise would help establish the number, locations, equipment types and operational details of hotspot resellers.

On 27 October, UCC reported that the committee’s first meeting brought together KACITA, hotspot resellers, mobile network operators, internet service providers and the Uganda Revenue Authority. The Commission identified existing public service provider licences, communal access models and agency arrangements with licensed operators as possible pathways for discussion. It said the committee would reconvene after two weeks to review progress.

At Parliament on 28 October, the Minister of ICT and National Guidance told the committee that the ministry had requested two weeks for further consultations and that the parties had agreed to a two-week period without arrests or confiscations while stakeholders were engaged. This was a statement recorded by Parliament during the hearing; it should not be confused with a permanent change to the licensing law.

A report published by UG Standard on 4 November added another important detail. It said UCC Executive Director George William Nyombi Thembo had ruled out licensing hotspot operators in their current informal form, saying they would need to change how they operate. The report described two routes mentioned by the regulator: qualifying for a public service provider licence or operating as agents of licensed companies.

As of 10 November, the public material reviewed for this article did not establish a final new licence category, a definitive fee schedule or a completed national registration outcome. The committee’s consultation work should therefore be treated as ongoing, not as a completed regulatory settlement.

## The law and the proposed pathways are different things

UCC’s 15 October statement refers to the Uganda Communications Act, 2013, and says a person may not provide telecommunications services to the public without a licence issued by the Commission.

The Act is the legal framework. The possible arrangements discussed in October are routes that may help eligible businesses comply with that framework. They are not, by themselves, proof that every informal hotspot reseller has been granted permission to continue operating.

This distinction is important for both operators and customers. A meeting, registration exercise or proposal does not automatically replace the authorisation required by law. Anyone entering or continuing in this business should seek written guidance from UCC and the relevant licensed network provider rather than relying on social media summaries or verbal assurances.

The regulator’s October discussions point towards a practical question: can a small reseller become an authorised agent of a licensed provider, or qualify for a licence that matches the scale and nature of the service? The final answer depends on the rules and commercial arrangements that the responsible institutions actually publish.

## What a fair framework should include

The following are recommendations, not announced UCC policy.

**1. Proportionate compliance routes.** A business serving a small defined area should not automatically face the same administrative burden as a national telecommunications provider. A clear agency route or a genuinely accessible community-scale authorisation could help smaller operators comply while keeping accountability with a licensed provider.

**2. Transparent fees and requirements.** Operators need to know the legal entity, registration documents, equipment standards, licence costs, renewal conditions and service obligations before they invest. Unclear or unaffordable requirements encourage uncertainty rather than compliance.

**3. Clear responsibility for customers.** A customer should be able to identify the licensed provider or authorised agent, understand the price and time limit of a bundle, receive a receipt where applicable, and know where to report poor service or misuse of personal information.

**4. Safe and approved installations.** Routers, cables and poles should not be attached to electricity or telecommunications infrastructure without the required permission. Equipment should meet applicable type-approval requirements, and installation practices should protect technicians, customers and the public.

**5. A predictable transition period.** If new arrangements are being designed, operators need a published timetable, a checklist, a way to ask questions and a clear explanation of what enforcement will apply during the transition. Any temporary pause should be communicated precisely so that businesses do not mistake it for permanent authorisation.

**6. Evidence-based public reporting.** UCC and the relevant associations should publish the registration results, explain how they count providers and distinguish operators from employees, equipment suppliers and individual access points. This would help Parliament and the public assess the scale of the sector more accurately.

## What customers should do while the issue is being resolved

Customers should not assume that every low-cost hotspot is authorised, but neither should they assume that every public Wi-Fi connection is automatically unsafe.

Before paying, ask who provides the service and whether the hotspot is operated by a licensed provider or an authorised agent. Check the advertised price, data or time limit, expiry conditions and complaint contact. If the operator cannot explain who is responsible for the connection, consider using a provider whose status can be verified through UCC.

Treat public Wi-Fi as a shared network. Avoid entering sensitive financial information on a connection you do not trust. Use secure websites and applications, keep device software updated, enable multi-factor authentication where available, and turn off automatic connection to unknown networks. For a transaction involving significant money or sensitive personal information, a trusted mobile-data connection may be the safer choice.

These are sensible precautions for public networks generally; they are not a claim that every hotspot involved in the current dispute has been compromised.

## What hotspot operators should do now

Operators should document their business model, locations, equipment and relationship with the upstream internet provider. They should contact UCC and the licensed provider supplying their connection to establish which authorisation or agency arrangement applies to their circumstances.

They should also avoid installing equipment on poles, buildings or other infrastructure without the owner’s permission, and should keep customer-facing prices, usage limits and support arrangements clear.

Most importantly, operators should not interpret the consultation process as a blanket licence to provide public telecommunications services. The safest course is to obtain written confirmation of the requirements that apply to their business.

## The real test is whether Uganda can protect access and accountability together

UCC has a responsibility to protect consumers, communications infrastructure and the integrity of the sector. Small businesses also have a legitimate interest in clear, affordable rules, particularly where their services help people connect in places where individual subscriptions may be less practical.

Those interests are not necessarily incompatible. A framework that identifies accountable licensed providers, sets proportionate conditions for agents, requires safe installations and gives customers a real complaints route could support both consumer protection and neighbourhood access.

The next important evidence will be the outcome of the committee’s work: the final licensing pathways, the actual costs, the registration results and the transition arrangements. Until those are published, claims that the dispute has been fully resolved—or that a new community licence is already available—would be premature.

Affordable connectivity matters. So do safety, accountability and lawful operation. Uganda’s public Wi-Fi policy should be judged by whether it can deliver all of them.

**What should a fair public Wi-Fi framework prioritise first: affordable access, consumer protection, infrastructure safety or the survival of small neighbourhood businesses?**

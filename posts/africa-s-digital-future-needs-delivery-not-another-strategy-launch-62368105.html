Africa’s Digital Future Needs Delivery, Not Another Strategy Launch

Africa’s Digital Future Needs Delivery, Not Another Strategy Launch

Africa does not lack digital ambitions. The continent has frameworks for digital transformation, data governance and artificial intelligence. What remains harder is turning those commitments into affordable connectivity, dependable infrastructure, useful public services, competitive local businesses and jobs.

That implementation gap was at the centre of the African Union’s Sixth Ordinary Session of the Specialised Technical Committee on Communication and ICT, whose ministerial segment took place in Libreville, Gabon, on 29 and 30 October 2026. In its 30 October statement, the African Union called for faster implementation of continental digital commitments, stronger investment and progress towards a Single African Digital Market.

The message is significant because it shifts attention from announcing strategies to delivering measurable benefits.

THE BUILDING BLOCKS ARE ALREADY IDENTIFIED

The AU’s Digital Transformation Strategy for Africa 2020–2030 provides a continental direction. The AU Data Policy Framework seeks more coherent data governance and interoperable systems. In 2024, the AU Executive Council endorsed a Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy that promotes an Africa-centred, development-focused approach to AI.

At Libreville, ministers and officials discussed how these commitments should connect: infrastructure, trusted digital ecosystems, AI, skills and innovation, strategic communications and Africa’s role in global digital governance. The AU also highlighted cross-border fibre, regional data centres, internet exchange points, cloud infrastructure and last-mile connectivity.

These are not separate technology projects. Fibre networks need reliable electricity. Digital services need secure identity and data systems. AI research needs computing capacity, relevant data and skilled people. Businesses need rules that allow them to serve customers across borders.

WHY A SINGLE DIGITAL MARKET MATTERS

A more integrated African digital market could make it easier for businesses to reach customers beyond their home countries, support cross-border digital trade and encourage services that work across national boundaries.

But a market cannot become integrated through declarations alone. Countries need compatible rules, practical cooperation on digital identity and data, secure electronic transactions, workable payment systems and mechanisms for resolving disputes. Businesses also need clarity about consumer protection, taxation, privacy and cybersecurity.

For a Ugandan software developer, for example, the opportunity is not simply to have a larger potential audience. It is to be able to onboard customers, receive payments, protect user data and meet regulatory requirements without facing a completely different technical and legal barrier in every market.

CONNECTIVITY MUST BE AFFORDABLE, NOT JUST AVAILABLE

New fibre routes and data centres are important, but their value depends on whether people and organisations can afford to use the services they enable. Rural communities, schools, clinics, small businesses and people with disabilities should be included in infrastructure planning from the beginning.

The AU’s 30 October statement linked digital investment to lower connectivity costs, expanded access, job creation, stronger African enterprises and improved public services. Those are useful measures because they focus on what citizens experience rather than counting infrastructure announcements.

Governments and partners should publish coverage, affordability and service-quality indicators, disaggregated where possible by location and underserved groups. A connection that is technically available but too expensive for a household or small business is not meaningful inclusion.

AI SHOULD SOLVE AFRICAN PROBLEMS AND BUILD AFRICAN CAPABILITY

The continent’s AI ambitions will be stronger if African researchers, businesses and public institutions can shape the tools they use. This includes developing systems that understand local languages and contexts, and applying AI to practical needs such as agriculture, health, education, climate resilience and public administration.

A 2 October 2026 announcement from UNDP and the GSMA described a partnership intended to support African-led AI innovation, including work on African-language AI solutions. Such initiatives can help connect local talent and industry networks, but lasting impact depends on sustained funding, access to computing resources, high-quality datasets and routes from research prototypes to products people can use.

AI adoption also needs safeguards. Public institutions should be able to explain when automated systems influence important decisions, assess bias and accuracy, protect personal information and keep meaningful human oversight where the consequences are significant.

THE IMPLEMENTATION TEST FOR GOVERNMENTS

The AU’s call for stronger financing and institutions should translate into practical national plans. Each country can identify a small number of high-impact priorities, assign responsible agencies, publish timelines and report progress openly.

A useful delivery framework would track at least five areas:

1. Access: Are internet services becoming more affordable and reliable, including outside major cities?
2. Public value: Are digital services reducing delays, paperwork and costs for citizens?
3. Local capacity: Are African firms, researchers and universities building and maintaining technology?
4. Trust: Are data protection, cybersecurity and accountability improving alongside digital access?
5. Opportunity: Are digital projects creating skills, decent work and markets for local businesses?

Regional cooperation should also produce shared standards and practical tools, not just meeting communiqués. Governments can learn from each other’s implementation results and avoid building incompatible systems repeatedly.

A MOMENT FOR AFRICAN EXECUTION

The AU meeting in Libreville did not create Africa’s digital agenda from scratch. It renewed the pressure to implement plans that already exist and to finance the infrastructure and institutions required to make them work.

For citizens, the test is straightforward: cheaper and more dependable access, public services that work, businesses able to grow across borders, useful local technology and stronger protection of digital rights.

Africa’s digital future will not be secured by the number of strategies adopted. It will be shaped by what governments, businesses, universities and communities build together—and by whether ordinary people can feel the difference.

Uganda’s National Data Strategy: The Real Test Begins After the Launch

Uganda’s five-year National Data Strategy promises better public services and a stronger data economy. Its success will depend on reliable infrastructure, privacy safeguards, measurable outcomes and transparent financing.

Uganda’s National Data Strategy: The Real Test Begins After the Launch

Uganda has a new national plan for how information should move through government, support public services and create opportunities for business. Launched in Kampala on 20 October 2026 by the Ministry of ICT and National Guidance, the National Data Strategy sets out an ambition that sounds simple but is difficult to deliver: make the country’s data more reliable, better protected and more useful.

The launch is important. But the real measure of success will not be the number of databases connected or the amount of information collected. It will be whether citizens experience better services, whether public institutions make better decisions and whether people can trust the systems holding their information.

WHAT THE STRATEGY IS TRYING TO CHANGE

The Ministry says the strategy is built around three pillars: data governance, data infrastructure and strategic data utilisation. People and culture, alongside research, innovation and entrepreneurship, are supporting areas.

The problem it addresses is familiar in many public institutions: information is collected in separate systems, formats and departments. When systems do not communicate, citizens can be asked for the same details repeatedly, officials struggle to build a complete picture and institutions may spend money collecting information that already exists elsewhere.

The Ministry says the strategy is intended to support secure information sharing, reduce duplication and improve evidence-based planning. It points to potential benefits in healthcare, education, water access, agriculture, tourism and other sectors. These are plausible opportunities, not outcomes that have already been achieved.

A FIVE-YEAR INVESTMENT THAT NEEDS PUBLIC ACCOUNTABILITY

The Ministry has described the implementation requirement as approximately UGX 1.6 trillion over five years. Uganda Broadcasting Corporation’s 22 October explainer reported a more specific estimate of about UGX 1.663 trillion and outlined proposed allocations across data infrastructure, governance, research and innovation, strategic data use and skills.

That figure should be read as a projected financing requirement, not proof that the full amount has already been secured or spent. The distinction matters. A strategy can have ambitious budgets on paper while projects face delayed procurement, changing priorities or gaps between planned and actual funding.

Government should therefore publish a clear implementation scorecard: what is funded, which institution is responsible, what has been delivered, what it cost and what measurable public benefit followed. Regular public reporting would make it easier for Parliament, civil society, journalists and citizens to distinguish progress from announcements.

PRIVACY MUST BE BUILT INTO THE INFRASTRUCTURE

Connecting data systems can make services more convenient, but it can also increase the consequences of poor access controls, weak security or unnecessary data collection. Information about health, education, identity and household circumstances can expose people to harm if it is accessed or shared improperly.

Uganda’s Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019 sets principles that include accountability, fair and lawful processing, limiting information to what is necessary, maintaining data quality, transparency and security safeguards. The strategy’s implementation should turn those principles into everyday technical and administrative practice.

That means clear rules about which institution may access which data, why access is needed, how long information is retained and how misuse is investigated. Systems should keep access logs, use strong authentication, encrypt sensitive information and provide practical ways for people to correct inaccurate records. Data sharing should not become unrestricted access.

INTEROPERABILITY IS MORE THAN CONNECTING DATABASES

A successful national data system needs common standards, accurate records and clear responsibility for correcting errors. Two systems can exchange information and still produce poor decisions if they use different definitions, outdated records or inconsistent identifiers.

For example, a local government planning a health programme needs dependable information about the communities it serves. If the source records are incomplete or not updated, a sophisticated dashboard will not solve the underlying problem. The quality of the decision remains limited by the quality of the data.

Uganda should begin with carefully selected services where better coordination can make a visible difference. Each project should identify the problem, the data needed, the responsible institutions, the privacy risks and the result citizens should expect.

CITIZENS SHOULD SEE THE BENEFIT

The strategy will be judged in ordinary moments: whether a person has to submit the same documents repeatedly, whether a clinic can plan supplies more accurately, whether local authorities can identify underserved communities and whether a small business can access reliable public information without unnecessary barriers.

Inclusion also requires attention to people who have limited connectivity, older devices, disabilities or lower digital literacy. A digital service that works only on fast internet and modern smartphones can make access worse for the people who need public services most. Offline and assisted channels must remain available where appropriate.

WHAT TO WATCH NEXT

The Ministry says implementation will be coordinated through a National Data Steering Committee and a National Data Office. Those structures will matter only if they have clear authority, skilled staff, public reporting and the ability to resolve coordination problems across institutions.

Uganda has taken a significant policy step. The next phase must turn the strategy into funded projects, trusted data practices and services that people can actually use. The strongest evidence of success will not be a larger national database. It will be less friction for citizens, better decisions by public institutions and more confidence that personal information is handled responsibly.
